OBSERVATION 01

Your business is running across (too?) many different platforms

I clocked Shopify for physical products, ThriveCart for courses, Skool for your community, and MonsterCampaigns for lead magnets. The problem isn't necessarily the tools themselves; it's that running a business through many separate platforms creates additional administrative work every time you launch something, manage a customer, or try to understand what's happening across your offers. Instead of technology giving you more leverage, it becomes another layer of work that grows alongside the business.

  • You're spending time managing multiple systems and workflows.
  • Launches and customer follow-ups can require coordination across different platforms.
  • The more offers you introduce, the more complicated the infrastructure can become.

RECOMMENDATION 01

Simplify the business infrastructure and connect the customer journey.

As your business grows, the systems behind it should make things easier, not create more work. You don't necessarily need to move everything into one platform, but you do need a more connected setup where your tools communicate, customer information flows between systems, and you can manage the business without constantly jumping between platforms. I'd evaluate GHL as the central funnel infrastructure, while keeping Shopify for physical product commerce.

  • Centralize landing pages, VSLs, application forms and CRM data.
  • Automate lead routing, follow-up and customer segmentation.
  • Use one central view to understand where each customer came from and what they've purchased.

RECOMMENDATION 02

Position the MBA around the economics of building a profitable mushroom business.

People don't wake up wanting to buy an accelerator. They want to build a business, create a new income stream, improve production, or stop making expensive mistakes. The page should start with those ambitions and show how the MBA helps them get there. Once visitors understand the opportunity and the cost of figuring it out alone, the program becomes a much more compelling investment.

  • Replace the broad headline with a more compelling promise tied to a meaningful business outcome
  • Use a VSL to explain the opportunity.
  • Strengthen the sales presentation with your methodology, proof, testimonials, program details, and a clear explanation of who the MBA is designed for.

OBSERVATION 03

There's no structured sales process turning MBA interest into qualified conversations

Your MBA form only collects a name and email, meaning you have no real understanding of who's interested before reaching out. You have to contact every inquiry individually just to discover their experience, business goals, current situation, and whether they're actually a good fit.

  • You're spending valuable time on people who may not qualify for the program.
  • You're entering sales conversations without knowing the prospect's needs or level of commitment.
  • Serious prospects may get the same follow-up process as people who are simply curious.

RECOMMENDATION 03

Qualify prospects before they ever reach your inbox.

Build a dedicated application funnel that collects the information you need upfront. This allows you to understand who's applying, prioritize the most promising opportunities, and spend your time speaking with people who are genuinely positioned to benefit from the program.

  • Replace the name-and-email form with qualification questions
  • Create a simple lead qualification process that separates serious applicants from people who aren't currently a good fit.
  • Connect qualified applicants to an automated calendar booking system, so they can schedule a sales conversation without requiring manual back-and-forth.

OBSERVATION 04

You're mixing two different markets: the hobbyist and the businessman

Your business has the opportunity to serve both the consumer market and the entrepreneur market, but those audiences require different positioning. Someone buying a kit wants an enjoyable and successful growing experience. Someone considering a mushroom farm wants to understand production, profitability, and how to build a viable business. Combining these messages can dilute the perceived relevance of your higher-value offers.

  • Hobbyist messaging can weaken the perceived business focus of your premium programs.
  • Entrepreneurs may not immediately recognize that you can help them build a commercial operation.
  • You're not fully leveraging the different needs and purchasing power of each audience.

If I were starting a backyard mushroom farm from zero, here's the exact order I'd do it in. Most beginners start with the wrong question. They ask "which mushroom should I grow?" when the real question is "who is going to buy it, and what does the setup need to cost?" Here's the sequence. 1. Start with the buyer. Chefs, farmers markets, local shops. Know who you're growing for before you grow anything. 2. Keep the first setup small. A garage corner is enough to learn the whole cycle. Don't build a farm before you've run a batch. 3. Learn one repeatable cycle. Inoculate, incubate, fruit, harvest. Run it until it's boring. 4. Fit it into a few hours a week. Simple routines and checklists. Your life doesn't pause for a farm. Most beginners skip step 1, then wonder why they have mushrooms and no customers. The goal isn't a bigger farm. It's a process you can repeat. Buyer. Setup. Cycle. Routine. That's the whole game.
